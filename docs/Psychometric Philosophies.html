2  Psychometric Philosophies

Measurement starts with the idea that anything that exists, exists to some degree, and therefore can be measured (Guion, 2011, p. 157)

The belief that we have the capability to measure the unseen and theoretical most likely started (at least widely acknowledged as starting) with Francis Galton, who predated “Psychology” but had an interest in individual differences (Galton, 1883). Later contributors to early Psychology held similar opinions (Edward Lee Thorndike, James Cattell), although some expressed skepticism (Alfred Binet).

Philosophies, within the context of Psychometrics, refer to theoretical beliefs regarding the nature of measurement. One of these beliefs is held tight, as an orthodoxy, such that empirical associations contrary to the orthodoxy are deemed heretical and violations of measurement (Rasch measurement – covered in Section 2.3). Most philosophies, however, take less extreme positions regarding the nature of measurement. It is useful for you to align yourself with one of these philosophical orientations from the perspective of assessment development. You may, however, borrow procedures from each of these traditions to evaluate the quality of your measure.

We will present four philosophical orientations, although these are not intended to represent a comprehensive or exhaustive listing. There are, no doubt, useful perspectives on the nature of measurement that have not yet been explicitly articulated or widely disseminated and the field is yet in need of perspectives that might help explain some persistently vexing mysteries:

There is nothing so practical as a good theory (Lewin, 1951)

Tradition Philosophy
CTT observed scores are attributable to person 'ability' and measure error
IRT probability of item endorsement is a function of person 'ability' & item characteristics
Rasch deviations from empirical prediction indicate poor measurement
Network Psychometrics manifest associations reveal substantive Psychological phenomena

2.1 Classical Test Theory

Although the same philosophical orientation is referred to in educational contexts as True Score Theory (TST), the Standards (as well as the broader social and behavioral sciences) have preferred to refer to this perspective on measurement as Classical Test Theory (CTT) (American Educational Research Association and American Psychological Association and National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014). This orientation has the longest history of specification and application and unquestionably remains dominant in layperson (e.g., non–Psychometrician) conceptualization of Psychological measurement.

The framework of CTT is built on three fundamental concepts:

  • T = “True” score
  • X = observed score
  • e = error

and the most concise specification of CTT simply specifies the algebraic form of relationship between these three concepts:

\[X = T + e \tag{2.1}\]

The observed score, \(X\), is the traditional focus of practical attention for any measurement practitioner. \(X\) is also referred to (synonymously) as a raw score. To best comprehend \(X\), you should also consider it to be synonymous with an aggregate scale score1. \(X\) is tangible. It is directly observable and quantifiable. When a student completes a 10–item mathematics exam and correctly answers 7 items, this student’s \(X\) is 72.

Of course the instructor interprets the student’s score, \(X\)=7, as an indication of the student’s knowledge of math. The student, however, may quibble with the interpretation and claim that their knowledge is greater3 – this brings us to a second CTT component, \(e\).

\(e\) represents the concept of error. There are two conceptually distinct types of error that CTT adherents acknowledge: random error (\(e_{r}\)) and systematic error (\(e_{s}\)).

The minimal CTT equation, \(X=T+e\), is most centrally concerned with random error, so we could rewrite the equation thusly: \[X = T + e_{r} \tag{2.2}\]

Equation 2.1 and Equation 2.2 are considered equivalent canonical4 specifications of CTT/TST. Guion (2011) has proposed a third alternative (Equation 2.3) that more explicitly accomodates \(e_{s}\), although this is NOT generally accepted as a canonical articulation of CTT. The (important) distinctions and similarities between \(e_{s}\) and \(e_{r}\), including the Guion (2011) reformulation of the CTT formula, will be discussed in XXXXX.

\[X = s + e \tag{2.3}\]

The third CTT component, \(T\), is a concept that only exists in the theoretical realm. \(T\) represents a person’s true score – their actual state of being with respect to the focal construct of interest5. For example, a student’s ACTUAL, REAL, TRUE knowledge of math.

\(T\) assumes that every person has a true location of construct standing along whatever construct is the focus of Psychometric attention. \(T\) is independent of any administered exam. We (mere mortal Psychometricians) have no special access to \(T\), we can only infer an individual’s \(T\) through behavioral indicators – one very germane indicator (we hope) is that individual’s score, \(X\), on a Psychological assessment.

To the extent that error, \(e\), is minimal, \(X\) will provide a good approximation of \(T\). If error grows, our inference that \(X\) is an adequate representation of \(T\) becomes more dubious. Of course as Psychometricians, we don’t often apply a word such as “dubious” (even though it is entirely appropriate and descriptive) – rather, we prefer, by tradition, more specific terms such as reliability and validity (these topics are covered in XXXXX and XXXXX).

2.2 Item Response Theory

A popular alternative to CTT focuses relatively greater philosophical attention to the item rather than the scale. Item Response Theory (IRT)

2.2.1 Dominance models

Dominance models, such as the GRM, assume a monotonically increasing relationship between the latent trait level and the probability of selecting a higher response category on an item (e.g., on a Likert scale). This contrasts with unfolding or ideal point models, where the response function is non-monotonic and peaks at a point matching the respondent’s trait level. 1–, 2–, 3–point logistic models (1PL, 2PL, 3PL), Graded Response Model, Partial Credit Model

2.2.2 Ideal point models

Frequently estimated via the Generalized Graded Unfolding Model [GGUM; Roberts et al. (2000)]
