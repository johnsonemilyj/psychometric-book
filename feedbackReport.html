Feedback report

The summarized results of administrations of your measure should be conveyed within a feedback report. These reports are intended to be delivered to assessment stakeholders, who are not expected to have any training in Psychometrics. The anticipated reading–level, by default, should be 7th grade (American standard), but you’ll also need to keep in mind anticipated mathematical literacy. Generally speaking, it is best to assume that your audience is borderline numerically illiterate – if you present numbers, make sure to also provide supplementary information (such as interpretation guides or training sessions).

“Stakeholders”

Individuals who have some level of interest in the administration and/or results of your measure are referred to as “stakeholders”. Any one stakeholder may hold multiple roles regarding your assessment. For example, often with Psychological assessment, stakeholders are both respondent and consumer, and these same individuals may or may not also be the purchasing agent (if your measure has been commercialized). Depending on the context, that same individual may also be the administrator, because many low–stakes Psychological assessments are self–administered.

Norms

Traditionally, the results of Psychological inventories are presented relative to a normative group (in the simplest scenario) or multiple normative groups (in a more complex scenario). Your feedback report is where your norms are most prominently significant.

In the earliest days of Psychological assessment, normative information was conveyed to the consumer in the form of static tables that contained:

  1. raw assessment scores, and
  2. standard score equivalents1

Contemporarily, your normative information is more commonly hidden within your scaled score algorithm and presented directly within the feedback report. Note that you should always carefully describe your normative sample within your technical report – your level of normative sample description within the feedback report will primarily depend on the needs of the consumer audience.

Report Styling

You should primarily keep your anticipated consumer in mind when designing your feedback report. Font size, density of information, and color schemes are all possibly impacted. Some feedback reports are volumnious (50+ pages) and some are concise. Online delivery is increasingly expected, and many design considerations can be customized via online delivery and the assistance of engines such as Section 1.4. Err on the side of “less is more”, especially with a first draft report.

Quarto

We are recommending Quarto as your technical and feedback report engine. You do not NEED to follow this recommendation – you can draw out a feedback report on the back of a cocktail napkin if you really want to do so – but we believe Quarto is a powerful tool to integrate data processing with semantic content within beautiful report formats (plus, we’re giving you templates to work with, so half your work is already done for you anyway)! Many assessment publishers have their own proprietary tools to create and deliver feedback to consumers, but Quarto is a great tool for independent assessment developers.

Please make sure to have Quarto installed on your computer prior to proceeding below (Quarto is an independent piece of software – it is NOT an R package).

Templates

Below are starter templates for individual (Section 1.5.1) and group (Section 1.5.2) reports. These are not exhaustive of all possible report foci (for example, 360° reports could be considered hybridized derivatives but fit neither category well). Rather, these should be considered starting points from which you can deviate and personalize (moreso in terms of functionality than style).

```{r scoring}

## focal person's scores

agree <- rowMeans(respondent[1:5], na.rm=TRUE) 
cons  <- rowMeans(respondent[6:10], na.rm=TRUE)
extra <- rowMeans(respondent[11:15], na.rm=TRUE)
neuro <- rowMeans(respondent[16:20], na.rm=TRUE)
open  <- rowMeans(respondent[21:25], na.rm=TRUE)

## Scale scores (μ=100, σ=15)

agree_ss <- round(((((agree-agree_mean)/agree_sd)*15)+100),0)
cons_ss  <- round(((((cons-cons_mean)/cons_sd)*15)+100),0)
extra_ss <- round(((((extra-extra_mean)/extra_sd)*15)+100),0)
neuro_ss <- round(((((neuro-neuro_mean)/neuro_sd)*15)+100),0)
open_ss  <- round(((((open-open_mean)/open_sd)*15)+100),0)

scores <- rbind(agree_ss, cons_ss, extra_ss, neuro_ss, open_ss)
dimension <- c("Agreeableness", 
               "Conscientiousness", 
               "Extraversion",
               "Neuroticism",
               "Openness")
toplot <- as.data.frame(cbind(dimension, scores))
colnames(toplot)[2] <- "Standard Score"

toplot$`Standard Score` <- as.numeric(as.character(toplot$`Standard Score`))
```

# Appendix

Reported scores should be interpreted relative to an average of 100 and standard deviation of 15. These values are extracted from a normative comparison group of `r nrow(norms)` men and women between the ages of `r min(norms$age)` and `r max(norms$age)` (average age = `r round(mean(norms$age),2)`, standard deviation = `r round(sd(norms$age),2)`, see @fig-norms).
